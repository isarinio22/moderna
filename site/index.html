Your tumor, your vaccine

Every cancer rewrites its own DNA as it grows. Those typos exist nowhere else in the body — which makes them the one thing that is unmistakably tumor and not you. This is how you turn them into a vaccine, and what the evidence so far actually shows.

Takes about 2 minutes Real tumor data Not medical advice

The idea

Cancer cells carry a fingerprint no healthy cell has

As a tumor grows it copies its DNA over and over, and it makes mistakes. Most of those mistakes do nothing. Some change the shape of a protein. Those changed proteins are called , and they are the only thing that marks a cancer cell as foreign.

Your immune system already patrols for foreign things. The problem is that it has never been shown what to look for, and the tumor is actively telling it to stand down. A personalized vaccine fixes the first problem. A second drug fixes the second.

Step one

Turning a typo into a wanted poster

Every cell constantly shreds its own proteins into short fragments and holds them up on its surface, like a shop displaying samples of everything inside. Passing inspect those samples. If a fragment looks wrong, the cell gets destroyed.

So a vaccine's job is to pick the fragments that span a mutation — the ones that look wrong — and teach the immune system their faces in advance.

Why it has to be personal

Your body can only display certain fragments

The molecules that hold fragments up for inspection are called , and yours are different from almost everyone else's. An HLA molecule is a particular shape, and it can only grip fragments that fit it. A mutation nobody in your body can display is useless as a target, however dramatic it looks in the DNA.

So the vaccine depends on two things: which mutations your tumor has, and which fragments your body can show. Here is the same tumor run against three different sets of common HLA types.

Step four

Writing them into one message

The chosen fragments get strung together into a single strand of — an instruction sheet. Your own cells read it, build the fragments, and display them. The immune system learns the faces without anyone ever having to inject a tumor.

The part that matters

Does it actually work?

A trial called KEYNOTE-942 gave 157 people with melanoma either this vaccine plus an antibody called pembrolizumab, or the antibody alone, after surgery removed their tumors. Then it waited five years to see who had a recurrence.
